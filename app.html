Don't know your wattage? Do the water test

The label on the back or inside the door lists it (use the output number, not input). No label? Physics can measure it: P = 4.187 x ml x temp rise / seconds.

698 watts (call it 700 W)
Temp rise10.0 °C
Energy absorbed41.9 kJ

Stir before measuring both temps, and use cool water (10-20 °C). Household heat loss makes this an estimate; treat it as plus or minus about 10%. Safety: plain water in a very smooth cup can superheat and erupt - put a wooden stir stick or microwave-safe spoon in the cup and don't overheat.

Cooking two portions?

Twice the food needs more energy, but not exactly twice the time (shape, surface and stirring all matter). Kitchen rule of thumb:

7:00 for 2 portions (x1.5)

Rough heuristic, not physics. Add the extra time, then check doneness early and finish in 30-second bursts.

Honesty corner

The formula keeps delivered energy constant: power x time on the package's oven equals power x time on yours. Real food still varies with mass, shape, starting temperature and container, so start with the converted time, check, and add short bursts. A reduced power level on most ovens means the magnetron cycles on and off - the pauses let heat spread inward, so for custards, casseroles and defrosting, keep the printed power level instead of converting it away.
